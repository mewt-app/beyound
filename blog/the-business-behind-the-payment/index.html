The Business Behind
the Payment

On a dashboard, a transaction is a line item. On the other side of it is a person who had a reason to make that payment — and that choice is where the real story begins.

By Kiran Prabhatan Director, BeyoundExtra Tech 26 August 2026 6 min read

A few months ago, if you had asked me what building a payments company would involve, I would probably have given you a fairly predictable answer. Product, technology, partnerships, operations, compliance, customer acquisition — all the usual pieces that come together to make a fintech work. What I didn't quite appreciate was how quickly all of those pieces start speaking to each other once you're actually building.

Today, a large part of my role at Rally sits across Finance and the business. That means I spend a lot of time looking at numbers: transaction volumes, revenue, costs, acquisition, retention, margins and everything in between. But the longer I spend doing it, the more I've realised that the most interesting numbers are rarely just numbers.

Take a transaction. On a dashboard, it is a line item. Someone paid a bill, a certain amount moved, a certain amount was earned. But on the other side of that transaction is a person who had a reason to make that payment. They had a bill due, a deadline to meet, or simply a payment they wanted to get out of the way. They chose Rally to do it. That choice is where the real story begins.

This is perhaps what I enjoy most about building Rally. You get to see the connection between the smallest customer decision and the larger business. A change in the product can change behaviour. A better customer experience can change retention. A pricing decision can change the economics of a transaction. A reward can bring someone back — or, if we haven't thought it through properly, simply become a cost with no lasting impact.

Nothing exists in isolation.

That makes Finance a much more interesting place to sit than people sometimes imagine. It isn't just about asking whether we can afford something. It's about understanding what we're buying with that investment. If we spend more to acquire a customer, are we acquiring someone who will transact once or someone who will stay with us? If we give RallyCash, are we creating a reason to come back? If we make a payment experience faster or simpler, does that actually change the way people use the product? The numbers don't answer those questions by themselves. They tell you where to look.

And payments make those questions even more important because the underlying product is money. When a customer makes a payment through Rally, we're asking them to trust us with something that matters to them. They want to know that the payment will go through, that their money will settle when we say it will, that the charges are clear and that they can find the details when they need them. These aren't features in the conventional sense. They're the foundations of trust.

I think that's also why the journey from acquiring a customer to retaining one is so fascinating. Getting someone to try Rally is one problem. Giving them enough reason to return is another. The first can be influenced by a campaign, an offer or a message. The second has much more to do with whether the product actually delivered on the promise.

We're now among the top five credit card bill payment applications in India, which is a milestone we're genuinely proud of. But being inside the business makes you look at that milestone differently. You don't spend too long celebrating the position before asking what it will take to maintain it. Growth is exciting, but sustainable growth is much more interesting. It means building a business where customers get more value, the product gets better, and the economics become stronger at the same time.

That balance is not always easy. There are decisions where the customer experience clearly wins, even if the immediate economics aren't perfect. There are other decisions where discipline matters more than excitement. Sometimes you make a bet because you believe the behaviour will change over time. Sometimes the numbers tell you to stop. And sometimes the hardest decision is admitting that something that looked good on paper simply isn't working in the real world.

Those are the moments when building a company becomes genuinely interesting. You're constantly moving between what the customer wants, what the product can deliver and what the business can sustainably support. The job isn't to maximise one of those things. It's to find the point where all three can work together.

That's probably the perspective I bring to Rally from Finance. I care deeply about the numbers, but I don't think the numbers are the destination. They are a way of understanding whether the things we're building are creating real value.

Because ultimately, a healthy business doesn't come from making a spreadsheet look good. It comes from giving people a reason to choose you, giving them an experience that makes them want to come back, and building the economics around that behaviour so you can keep improving the product.

That's what we're trying to do at Rally.

We're building a payments business, but we're also building the habits, trust and economics that can make it last.

And for me, that's the most exciting part of the journey.
