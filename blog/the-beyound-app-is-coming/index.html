Beyound Was Built on the Web.
The App Is Built on Behaviour.

Six months of shipping on the web didn't delay the app — it earned us the right to build it properly. A note on what we kept, what we cut, and what we're letting the phone finally do.

By Ambalika Roy Director, BeyoundExtra Tech 20 May 2026 6 min read

When we launched Beyound on the web earlier this year, it wasn't because we didn't believe in mobile. It was because we wanted to earn the right to build the app properly.

The web gave us something most teams skip: clarity.

We could see where users hesitated. What they repeated. Which flows became habits. Which ones never did. We could ship faster, observe harder, and avoid locking ourselves into assumptions disguised as product strategy.

Six months later, the data is no longer ambiguous.

Bill payments — the BBPS side of Beyound — already belong to the phone. Nearly 83% of bill-pay sessions this year came from mobile browsers. Wallet loading and transfers leaned the other way: about 60% desktop, 40% mobile, with many users starting on desktop and completing transfers later on their phones.

That changed the question completely.

We're not building an app to replace the website. We're building an app for the parts of the product the phone naturally wins at — while letting the desktop continue doing what it's objectively better at.

That distinction matters. Most companies force behaviour into platforms. We'd rather follow behaviour instead.

What We Refused to Change

A surprising amount of the product survived untouched. Not because we lacked ideas — because users had already voted.

The first non-negotiable was login simplicity. No passwords. No "sign in with Google." No email magic links pretending to be modern. Just your phone number, a six-digit OTP, and access.

If you've used Beyound once on the web, the app immediately inherits your world: beneficiaries, KYC tier, payment history, pinned billers, everything. No migration ceremony. No "set up your account again" friction masquerading as onboarding.

The second thing we kept was the BBPS category system. Electricity. Mobile. Broadband. Gas. Insurance. Credit Cards. The entire structure already worked. We experimented with redesigns — cleaner grids, more "app-like" layouts. Users rejected both. So we stopped trying to out-design muscle memory.

And then there's the receipt. This sounds small until you realise trust in financial products often lives in the smallest details.

Every payment still ends with the Bharat Connect receipt: transaction ID, B Assured stamp, tax split, exact-match invoice copy. People forward these to landlords. Submit them to finance teams. Save them for reimbursements. Send them to their CA.

The receipt is one of the highest-trust surfaces in the entire product. We treated it accordingly.

Most companies force behaviour into platforms. We'd rather follow behaviour instead. — A note we taped above the design Jira board, March 2026

The Bigger Shift: Fewer Screens, More Intent

The hardest part of mobile design isn't adding features. It's deciding what deserves to survive.

The desktop dashboard didn't make the cut. On the web, dashboards work because large screens reward context. On phones, users don't arrive looking for summaries — they arrive looking to complete something. So the first screen after login is action-first, not information-first.

We also collapsed complexity aggressively. The web product currently has 84 routed pages. The app has 12 screens. That reduction wasn't cosmetic. It was philosophical.

If something only required a quick interaction — a toggle, a one-field edit, a confirmation — it became a sheet, not an entire destination. The result feels lighter because it actually is lighter.

We didn't shrink the website into a phone. We rebuilt the product around intent density.

The Phone Finally Gets to Be a Phone

The web is powerful, but it's blind to hardware. Mobile changes that.

  • Biometric-gated transfers. Transfers above configurable thresholds use Face ID or fingerprint instead of repeatedly asking for an MPIN.
  • Camera-first add-money. Point your phone at your card; on-device OCR prefills the fields and continues. The image never leaves the device.
  • Bill auto-detect from SMS. Standard Indian telecom and electricity SMS patterns are detected locally and surfaced into the payment flow. Entirely opt-in. Entirely local-first.
  • Offline receipts. Every successful payment is cached locally, because "no network" has a habit of appearing exactly when proof of payment matters most.

None of these features exist for novelty. They exist because mobile devices should contribute capabilities — not just screen size.

One Product. One Source of Truth.

Underneath all of this, the architecture stays intentionally boring. Same APIs. Same wallet infrastructure. Same KYC systems. Same Bharat Connect rails.

The app is not a parallel product. It's another window into the same account, the same balance, the same transaction graph.

That sounds obvious until you've seen what happens when companies split logic between platforms. Compliance drifts. Permissions disagree. States desynchronise. Maintenance compounds.

We wanted none of that. The server remains the authority for everything that matters: wallet limits, KYC permissions, RBI constraints, BBPS enforcement.

The app's job is not to invent reality. Its job is to render it beautifully and instantly.

Where We Are Now

The app is currently in beta with roughly 400 users: early customers, internal teams, and a small group of power users who spend enough time inside the product to break things faster than we can predict them.

Public launch for iOS and Android is targeted for the end of June 2026.

And honestly, this launch feels different from our previous ones. Not because it's bigger — because it's more certain. The web product taught us what people actually wanted Beyound to become. The app is simply the first version of us responding properly.

There's more coming after this — deeper work around OCR reliability, offline receipt verification, and the reasoning behind several design decisions the team argued over far longer than we'd like to admit.

But for now: thank you. Every tap, failed flow, repeated payment, support ticket, and late-night transaction shaped this product more than any roadmap ever could.
