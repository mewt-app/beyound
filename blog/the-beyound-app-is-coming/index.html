The Beyound app is coming. Here's what we're keeping, what we're cutting, and why.

Six months of building the wallet on the web taught us which flows belong in your thumb and which were never going to make it past a desktop browser. A note on the trade-offs.

By Ambalika Roy Director, BeyoundExtra Tech 20 May 2026 6 min read

The web flow is the brief. When we launched the Beyound wallet on the web at the start of the year, we made a deliberate choice: ship where the rails were strongest, see what people did, and only then build the mobile experience the data asked for. Six months in, the data has opinions.

What it told us: bill payments — the BBPS half of Beyound — happen almost entirely on phones. 83% of bill-pay sessions on bills.beyound.in this year came from a mobile browser. Wallet load and onward transfer split the other way: roughly 60% desktop, 40% mobile, with a long tail of users who load on desktop and then transfer on mobile a day later.

So we're not building one app to replace the website. We're building an app that picks up the half of the product the phone is already winning, and leaves the desktop browser to keep doing the half it's better at. That's the headline. Here are the trade-offs underneath it.

What we're keeping

The non-negotiables came out of looking at session recordings, not a roadmap. Three patterns showed up over and over.

One — the OTP-only login. No password, no magic-link email, no "sign in with Google." A phone number, an OTP, and you're in. The Beyound twilio integration delivers a 6-digit code; verifying it mints a session that the same merchant ID carries across web and app. If you've used the wallet once on the web, your beneficiaries, KYC tier, payment history and pinned billers all show up on the app the moment you sign in. Nothing to re-do.

Two — the categorisation we already ship. The 30 BBPS categories — Electricity, Mobile, Broadband, Gas, Insurance, Credit Card and the long tail — render on the home screen the same way they render on bills.beyound.in: grouped, scrollable, searchable. We tried two redesigns of this grid early on, ran both past users, and both lost to the existing layout. So we kept it.

Three — the receipt. Every payment ends with the Bharat Connect receipt: B-Connect Txn ID, B Assured stamp, CGST/SGST split for the convenience fee, an exact-match copy of what we send to your inbox. People print these. People email them to landlords. People send them to their CA. The receipt is the product surface that earns the most trust per pixel; we're not touching it.

The job isn't to pack the website into a phone-shaped box. It's to ask, screen by screen, what the phone changes about that screen — and only then redraw the ones where the answer is "something." — A note we taped above the design Jira board, March 2026

What we're cutting

Cuts are harder than additions, mostly because everyone who shipped the feature you're cutting is still in the room. Here's what didn't make the app and why.

The desktop dashboard. The web wallet has a KPI-style dashboard above the fold — balance, monthly spend, pending KYC, three "pinned" transfers. On a phone, all of that moves into the navigation, not the surface. The first screen after login on the app is the action you came to take, not a summary of actions you've already taken.

Multi-tab beneficiary management. On the web you can have your beneficiary list open while you fill the transfer form; you can compare two beneficiaries side by side before deciding. The phone doesn't do that. So the app drops the management list as a destination and instead promotes beneficiaries to selection chips inside the transfer flow itself. The full list still exists, but you don't visit it — you just pick from your last five at the top of the payment screen.

The "deep links every screen has its own URL" model. The web has 84 routed pages. The app has 12 screens. The collapse is on purpose. If a feature on the web is only one level deep — a settings toggle, a single-field edit — it becomes a sheet in the app, not a screen. We bought back about 40% of our screen-count budget by being strict about this.

What changes because the device changes

A few features only exist because the phone gives us hardware the web doesn't.

  • Biometrics gating the wallet transfer. Face ID or fingerprint instead of a re-entered MPIN for any transfer over a configurable threshold. The web equivalent (WebAuthn) works, but adoption is roughly 1.5% — on the app it'll be closer to default.
  • Camera-first add-money. If your card is in hand, point at it. We don't store the image; we OCR it on device, prefill the four fields, then discard. The card never leaves the phone.
  • Bill auto-detect from SMS. Same idea, applied to the standard Indian telecom and electricity bill SMS formats. The app reads the next-due amount and one-taps you into the payment flow. (Opt-in. Off by default.)
  • Offline receipt. Every successful payment gets cached locally so you can pull up a receipt without reception. Useful at toll plazas, useful at petrol pumps, useful basically anywhere "no network" hits at the wrong time.

What stays exactly as it was

The backend doesn't move. Every API the app hits is the same API the web hits. Same auth, same wallet, same Zokudo wrapper for KYC, same Bharat Connect rails for BBPS. If the app is a window onto the same product the web is — and conceptually that's what we want it to be — then there's only one definition of "your account" and one definition of "your transactions." Anything else is a maintenance trap.

The compliance footprint also doesn't move. RBI prepaid wallet rules, NPCI BBPS rules, KYC tier limits, the grievance redressal matrix — all of it is enforced server-side. The app just renders what the server says is allowed. We didn't want a world where the app and the web disagreed about whether a user could top up; the simplest way to avoid it is to never let the app be the source of truth for those answers.

When you'll see it

We're in beta with about 400 users — friends-of-the-team, a handful of early customers, and the BBPS ops folks who basically live in the product anyway. Public launch on iOS and Android is targeted for end of June 2026.
